\subsubsection{notes from: A meta-analytic review on incorporating virtual and augmented reality in museum learning \cite{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}}
-  This superimposing digital augmentation effect often occurs through a camera view with mobile platforms or a
see-through display such as smart glasses (Pellas et al., 2019; Scavarelli et al., 2021). For example, a mobile device camera captures
and recognizes a real-world object and then overlaps this object or its digital representation with extra text, audio, videos or other
virtual elements
\\
- In the study Yoon et al. (2017), when a physical ball is floating in the front of a Bernoulli
blower, an AR device presents a digital image of the moving air around a digital ball that has been generated by the AR device as a
digital representation of the physical ball; that is, visitors can see both the physical ball and the digital ball with a superimposed image
of moving air. In other situations, AR devices directly project digital virtual objects in the physical space, e.g., displaying a virtual
volcano in the classroom.
\\
- Therefore, in museum learning, AR can provide learners easy access to unobservable phenomena or digital
information on exhibits and promote their understanding of the real world
\\
- The theoretical foundation of incorporating VR and AR in museum learning is grounded in experiential learning (Behrendt &
Franklin, 2014; Kolb, 2014), situated learning (Falk & Dierking, 2000; Johnson, 2002), active learning (Prince, 2004), and the
multimedia learning theory (Mayer, 2014; Mayer & Estrella, 2014).
\\
- Experiential learning typically involves four interactive learning
processes: concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation (Kolb, 2014). Learners
gain deeper understanding of the learning contents through these learning processes in authentic and first-hand context (Behrendt &
Franklin, 2014; Kolb, 2014).
\\
- Similar to experiential learning, situated learning involves learning within the context in which
knowledge is situated and it might be used and applied (Choi & Ahn, 2021; Johnson, 2002). Active learning refers to learners’ active
engagement in meaningful learning activities and their reflection on learning (Chiang et al., 2021; Prince, 2004). Multimedia learning
theory emphasizes the presentation of text and relevant graphical illustrations to promote learners’ cognitive processing. Recently,
researchers have expanded multimedia learning theory by incorporating emotional designs that can motivate learners to exert more
mental effort to make sense of the presented material (Mayer, 2020; Mayer & Estrella, 2014). There is no doubt that VR and AR
technologies are able to combine multiple media contents (such as texts, audio, video), provide an interactive, engaging educational
context to support experiential and situated learning in museums, and induce learners to search more information for meaning-making
and understanding, thus promoting active learning (e.g., inspecting historical settlements of ancient society) (Huang et al., 2016;
\\
- However, museums are currently suffering from reduced interest and lower attendance among the younger generations (Crowley
et al., 2014). Thus, it is important to improve visitors’ interest by designing engaging learning experiences (Hassan & Ramkissoon,
2016). Existing review studies on museum learning have focused on the adoption of technology in museums. Wang et al. (2017)
reviewed the studies on ubiquitous language learning using location-based systems in museums from the perspective of system usefulness,
activity usefulness and activity playfulness; they found that ubiquitous location-based systems using mobile devices helped to
promote students’ collaboration in group learning activities. Lin et al. (2021) investigated the trends in research on museum-based
mobile learning in terms of research methods, learning domains, location-based technologies, learning strategies, and research
topics. Xu et al. (2021) conducted a meta-analysis analyzing 42 experimental or quasi-experimental studies on technology applications
in museum learning published during 2011–2021 and showed that technology application has a moderate or high effect on museum
learning. In recent years, AR and VR are increasingly used to create engaging activities for learners (Falomo Bernarduzzi et al., 2021;
Scavarelli et al., 2021).
\\
-For example, in Chang et al. (2014), a
mobile AR guide system presented paintings in a more instinctive way, aiming to foster college students’ appreciation for painting.
Moreover, AR has also been used in medical museums to display virtual labels, images and natural interfaces to advance students’
understanding of medical specimens (Sugiura et al., 2019).
\\
- The learning outcomes measured in existing studies vary and generally include knowledge achievement, thinking skills, individuals’
affections, etc. For example, Yoon et al. (2017) found that even when given only a short period for investigation in a science
museum, students using AR demonstrated significantly greater knowledge gains than those who did not. Some studies have reported
that AR/VR-assisted museum learning promotes learners’ higher-order thinking skills, such as creative thinking (Guazzaroni, 2013;
Hammady et al., 2020), inquiry (Hsiao et al., 2016), and critical thinking skills (Poce et al., 2019). Some studies have investigated
learners’ motivation and emotions. For example, Dieck et al. (2018) and Puig et al. (2020) revealed that these technologies enhance
visitors’ satisfaction and enjoyment and that wearable equipment helps to personalize their learning experience. Additionally, Nechita
and Rezeanu (2019) reports that a multisensory augmented museum space can enhance empathy by allowing learners to experience
various historical circumstances from a first-hand perspective.
However, other studies found negative effects of AR/VR-assisted museum learning. For example, Savela et al. (2020) found that AR
games in a science center did not necessarily improve learning performance or social interaction when compared to the traditional
pen-and-paper learning method. Additionally, learners claimed that using VR devices made them feel nauseous and dizzy (Rhee,
2019), and some devices such as HMDs also physically inconvenienced visitors (Oh et al., 2018; Sugiura et al., 2019). Therefore,
whether and to what extent AR and VR applications could benefit visitors in museum learning are questions that still need to be
synthesized and clarified.
\\
- The results for the categorization of design elements can be seen in the upper part of Fig. 6. Based on the coding framework
presented in Table 2, 23 studies used AR or VR to superimpose supplementary information onto physical exhibits to explain the
exhibition content (e.g., Tabone, 2020). The superimposed information was in the form of labels or texts (Juan et al., 2017, pp.
466–468; Tabone, 2020), images, audio and video (Damala et al., 2016), or 3D models (Borovanska et al., 2020; Connaghan et al.,
2019; Ghouaiel et al., 2017). This could be seen as a similar function of labels used for traditional exhibits.
\\

\\
- In addition to augmenting visitors’ view, one prominent affordance of AR and VR is to allow for user interaction with virtual
objects. Among the selected studies, visitors were able to manipulate virtual objects (3), including manipulating 3D objects to observe
weather variations with an AR app (Hsiao et al., 2016), performing quiz-based tasks (4), and generating content (4), such as constructing
Cycladic figurine with stony tools used in ancient times (Koutsabasis & Vosinakis, 2018). In some studies (e.g., Dudzik, 2018;
Jung et al., 2016; Puig et al., 2020), the design elements were more than one when an article contained two or more studies that used
different design elements to support various learning activities.
\\
- First, mobile devices were often used to superimpose supplementary information onto physical
exhibits (19). Visitors could use smartphones or tablets to scan exhibits or QR codes or download applications to obtain exhibit-related
knowledge in the form of digital text and multimedia information overlaid on physical objects. For example, visitors could view and
listen to the corresponding commentary about paintings after capturing the paintings with smartphone cameras (Chang et al., 2014).
Second, simulations of invisible phenomena were implemented using several types of devices including desktops (4), projectors (2),
and HMDs (1). Simulation of exhibitions without the presence of physical objects was implemented with HMDs (3) and mobiles (3).
\\
-Third, quiz game-based learning tasks were all performed on mobile devices (4). For example, in Savela et al. (2020), visitors used a
cellphone AR app to visualize the virtual floating question marks around related exhibits and must tour the entire exhibition room to
answer all the quiz questions.
\\
-Links between specific devices and AR and VR technologies. The left bubble chart in Fig. 7 depicts how a specific device was
used (i.e., for augmentation or total immersion). It shows that mobile devices such as smartphones and tablets were mostly used for AR
(28). Visitors can view digital augmentations through applications installed on phones or tablets (Borovanska et al., 2020; Connaghan
et al., 2019).
HMDs were equally used for AR (8) and VR (8). For AR, HMDs were mainly low immersion headsets such as Google Cardboard and
Google Glass (Tabone, 2020; Yu et al., 2019); that is, users could see the physical world rather than being totally surrounded with
computers-generated images and sound. HMDs for VR, such as HTC VIVE or Samsung Gear VR Glasses (Baradaranrahimi et al., 2020,
p. 1; Li & Chang, 2017; Puig et al., 2020), allowed users to experience a high degree of immersion and provided a complete sense of
presence. Desktop devices (i.e., nonmovable computers or equipment in museums) were mainly used for AR (5) that displays digital
augmentations after visitors interacted with exhibits (Yoon et al., 2018; Yoon & Wang, 2014).
\\
- A total of 17 studies met all of the inclusion criteria for RQ3 and examined the effects of AR/VR-assisted versus non-AR/VR-assisted
learning on visitors’ individual academic achievement and perceptions. Table 4 presents detailed information to address RQ3 and the
95% confidence interval for each outcome. Note that only 1 out of the 17 studies investigated the effects of VR with non-VR supported
museum learning; thus, the effect size of AR and VR were not calculated separately.
\\
- The weighted mean effect size for academic achievement synthesized from 13 studies was 0.45 (95% CI [0.00, 0.89], k =13),
indicating a significant positive effect of AR and VR on visitors’ measured gains in knowledge and skills. In other words, visitors using
AR and VR applications in museum learning had significantly better academic achievement than those who used non-AR and non-VR
applications such as text or traditional audio guides
\\
- Individual effect sizes of the 13 primary studies ranged from − 1.30 to 2.05.
Sommerauer and Müller (2014), for example, examined the effect of AR in learning mathematical content through AR exhibitions. The
experiment was conducted with 101 participants (from the general public) for 90 min in an anonymous national museum. All of the
participants received hands-on training on how to use the mobile AR app and were randomly assigned to experimental or control
groups. The results showed that participants in the experimental group outperformed their counterparts on questions related to AR
exhibits, with an ES of 0.75.
\\
- The weighted mean effect size for perceptions synthesized from 12 studies was 0.59 (95% CI [0.29, 0.91], k =12), indicating a
medium effect of AR and VR on visitors’ perceptions. The individual effect sizes of the 12 primary studies ranged from − 0.21 to 2.19.
For example, Chang et al. (2014) implemented AR with a mobile device as a guide system for art appreciation in the Taipei Fine Arts
Museum. The experiment was conducted among a sample of 131 college students. The participants in the experimental group who used
AR-guided systems reported a better state of flow experience and were more proactive in appreciating the artwork than groups using
audio only or groups without any guide.
\\
- In addition, AR devices are cost-effective and easy to use compared to VR devices (e.g., HMDs), which are not as
prevalent in museums currently.
\\
- The positive effects of AR/VR-assisted museum learning (ES =0.45 for academic achievement; ES =0.59 for positive perceptions)
can be explained by the fact that AR and VR provide enriching and immersive learning settings, as experiential or situated learning
have proved to promote learning (Kolb, 2014). Learners can be actively involved in cognitive processing when a coherent mental
representation of their own experiences is constructed (Mayer, 2014)
\\
- Based on the above findings, suggestions for the design of future research on AR/VR-assisted museum learning are as follows:
(1) Extend the learning content in museums. A majority of the current studies focused on declarative knowledge learning, such as
general information regarding the artists and their works, while few were related to procedural-practical knowledge and distal
outcomes such as analytical and other thinking skills. Therefore, this learning content could be the learning objectives of future
studies.
(2) Provide real immersive contexts. The design of museum learning environments should make full use of VR technologies to
provide real immersive learning environments. Instead of merely providing animation on desktop devices with low immersion,
the learning settings should make more use of VR technologies. This requires collaboration between museum education designers
and technological experts.
(3) Take into account the cognitive levels of visitors at different ages. The cognitive abilities and usage of technologies and devices
vary among visitors of different ages, and their preferences concerning museums visits are also diverse. Thus, future research
should consider the differences among visitors to meet different visiting requirements.
(4) Employ controlled experimental design. It is typical that many of the selected 51 empirical studies focused on the usability of AR
and VR technologies and were conducted among a single group of participants without controlled experiments. In this review,
only 17 out of the 51 selected articles used a controlled experimental design. More experimental or quasi-experimental studies
with a control group design are needed to investigate the effects (such as the acquisition of knowledge and skills) of using AR
and VR technologies in museum learning.
\\
- Third, this review does not examine the barriers to the usage of AR and VR for museum learning, as some papers
have reported common disadvantages, such as dizziness and device burden, which may be a factor influencing the learning effect.
Fourth, the samples for the meta-analysis were relatively small.
\\
- This work presents a systematic literature review and meta-analysis on the contexts of using AR and VR technologies to support
museum learning, design elements of how AR and VR are incorporated into the learning activities, and the effects of AR/VR-assisted
museum learning on academic achievement. First, the results indicate that AR and VR have mostly been used by K-12 students in
science and art museums, mainly for acquiring declarative knowledge. Second, AR and VR are often used to superimpose supplementary
information onto physical exhibits, dynamically visualize typically invisible phenomena or concepts, and simulate virtual
exhibition and narrative scenarios. Only a few studies have used AR and VR to improve visitors’ interaction with exhibitions by
allowing them to manipulate or generate virtual objects. Finally, this meta-analysis reveals that AR and VR applications have produced
favorable effects on both academic achievement (ES =0.45) and learner perceptions (ES =0.59). Our findings may therefore
contribute to a deeper understanding of AR/VR-assisted learning in museums.
\\
\subsection{Notes for the Design of VIRTUE, Interactive Stations, Screen and the Questionnaire}

\subsubsection{General Design Principles}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Low threshold for interaction:}
    Each station should immediately communicate what visitors can do.
    The first interaction should be simple and provide an incentive
    to continue exploring.
    \cite[p.~139]{hornecker_learning_2006} \cite[p.20]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Layering of activities:}
    Each station should offer different levels of engagement.
    Visitors with little time should be able to complete a simple
    activity, while interested visitors can explore additional
    information or interactions.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Different visitor interests:}
    The exhibition should combine different types of activities,
    including physical objects, interactive digital content,
    historical information and exploratory experiences.
    \cite[pp.~138--139]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Active participation:}
    Visitors should be encouraged to perform meaningful activities
    rather than only read information.
    Hands-on installations were particularly successful in
    attracting visitors from different age groups.
    \cite[pp.~139--140]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Group interaction:}
    Interactive stations should allow visitors to collaborate,
    discuss and observe each other's interactions.
    \cite[pp.~140--142]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Complete visitor journey:}
    The exhibition should be designed as a complete visitor journey,
    including preparation before VR, the immersive experience itself,
    and the transition back into the physical environment.

    Whittaker argues that onboarding and offboarding should
    not be considered only within the headset.
    \cite[p.~93]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{User-centred VR design:}
    The exhibition should accommodate visitors with different
    levels of VR experience, expectations and physical abilities.

    The onboarding procedure should not assume that every
    visitor is already familiar with immersive technology.
    \cite[pp.~101--103]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Physical environment matters:}
    The spatial arrangement, furniture, privacy and atmosphere
    of the exhibition should support the intended VR experience.

    The physical environment influences how visitors experience
    the transition into the virtual environment.
    \cite[p.~100]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Open-ended exploration:}
    Allow visitors to explore selected exhibits without having
    to follow a strictly predefined sequence of instructions.

    Franse et al. observed that families were able to conduct
    meaningful investigations and discuss their observations
    even without guidance.
    \cite[pp.~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Minimal and optional guidance:}
    Provide support when visitors need it, without unnecessarily
    restricting their freedom to explore.

    In the study by Franse et al., both describing evidence and
    giving explanations reduced certain incorrect interpretations.

    This suggests combining independent exploration with
    additional educational support.
    \cite[pp.~727,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Encourage visitor discussion:}
    Design activities that encourage visitors to compare
    observations, formulate ideas and discuss possible explanations.

    Families in the study engaged in meaningful conversations
    while investigating the open-ended exhibit.
    \cite[pp.~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Balance freedom and structure:}
    Visitors should have meaningful choices, but activities
    should remain manageable and understandable.

    The exhibit studied by Franse et al. offered multiple
    experimental possibilities while deliberately limiting
    the number of variables and combinations.
    \cite[p.~728]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Active and experiential learning:}
    The exhibition should encourage visitors to actively
    explore Fasnacht objects rather than only consume
    written information.

    Zhou et al. discuss experiential, situated and active
    learning as theoretical foundations for AR and VR
    in museum education.
    \cite[p.~2]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Meaningful use of multimedia:}
    Combine text, images, audio, video and interactive
    3D content where these media help visitors understand
    the exhibition topics.

    The review describes the use of supplementary
    multimedia information to enrich physical exhibits.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Clear learning objectives:}
    Define what visitors should learn from each station,
    such as recognizing Fasnacht figures, identifying
    instruments or understanding cultural traditions.

    The majority of the reviewed studies focused
    on declarative and conceptual knowledge.
    \cite[pp.~8,~12]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Accessibility for different age groups:}
    Design activities that can be understood by visitors
    with different ages, prior knowledge and levels
    of technological experience.

    Zhou et al. recommend considering differences
    in cognitive abilities and technology use among
    museum visitors.
    \cite[p.~13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Technology should support the content:}
    AR and VR should provide a meaningful educational
    extension of the exhibition rather than being
    included only because they are novel.

    This is a proposed design principle based on the
    review's focus on learning objectives and
    technology affordances.
    \cite[pp.~5--6,~12]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Interactive Stations in the Waiting Area}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Station 1 -- Fasnacht figures:}
    Display verses describing different traditional Fasnacht
    figures on a physical poster. Visitors can scan a QR code
    to access VirtuLearn, match verses with the corresponding
    figures and discover their cultural background.

    This provides a simple initial task with optional deeper
    exploration.
    \cite[pp.~139--141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Station 1 -- Independent discovery:}
    Visitors should be able to explore and compare the
    different Fasnacht figures before receiving their
    names and background information.

    For example, visitors could first examine the masks
    and form an idea about which verse belongs to which
    figure.

    This is a design adaptation of the open-ended
    investigation approach described by Franse et al.
    \cite[pp.~708,~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Station 1 -- Explanations after exploration:}
    After attempting to match a verse with a figure,
    visitors should receive a short explanation of the
    figure's characteristics and cultural background.

    The intention is to preserve the discovery activity
    while preventing visitors from leaving with
    incorrect interpretations.

    This decision is inspired by the positive effects
    of minimal explanatory guidance on reasoning content.
    \cite[pp.~727,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Station 1 -- Augmented Fasnacht figures:}
    Allow visitors to access additional information
    about selected Fasnacht figures through VirtuLearn.

    Visitors could inspect 3D models and use AR
    to place digital representations of the masks
    in their physical surroundings.

    This is a proposed application of supplementary
    digital information and virtual object interaction.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Station 1 -- Quiz-based learning:}
    The matching activity between Fasnacht verses
    and figures could be implemented as a short
    interactive quiz within VirtuLearn.

    Quiz game-based learning tasks were one of
    the design elements identified in the review,
    including four studies using mobile devices.
    \cite[pp.~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- Fasnacht music:}
    Present a physical poster introducing the musical
    traditions of Basler Fasnacht.

    The poster should explain the differences between
    traditional Cliquenmusik and Guggemusik.

    Cliquenmusik is characterized by the combination
    of Piccolo and Basler Trommel, while Guggemusik
    typically uses brass and percussion instruments.

    Short bilingual explanations and photographs
    should provide visitors with an introduction
    to the cultural significance of Fasnacht music.

    This is a proposed application of layered
    information in museum exhibitions.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}


    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- Interactive instrument exploration:}
    Provide a QR code linking to the music station
    within VirtuLearn.

    Visitors can choose between the Piccolo and
    the Basler Trommel and listen to individual
    audio recordings.

    Both recordings should be accessible independently,
    allowing visitors to compare the characteristic
    sounds of the instruments.

    The interaction should remain open-ended,
    without requiring visitors to complete a quiz
    or follow a predefined sequence.

    This approach is inspired by the importance
    of active visitor participation in interactive
    museum installations.
    \cite[p.~140]{hornecker_learning_2006}


    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- Comparing sounds:}
    Encourage visitors to independently explore
    the differences between the Piccolo and
    Basler Trommel.

    Visitors should be able to play the recordings
    repeatedly and in any order.

    A short question on the physical poster could
    encourage reflection, for example:

    "How do the melody and rhythm differ between
    the two instruments?"

    Visitors are not required to provide an answer.
    Instead, the activity encourages observation
    and independent exploration.

    This is a proposed adaptation of open-ended
    investigation to the context of musical instruments.
    \cite[pp.~708,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}


    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- 3D and AR exploration:}
    After selecting an instrument in VirtuLearn,
    visitors can choose between viewing it in
    3D or exploring it using augmented reality.

    The 3D viewer allows visitors to rotate,
    zoom and examine the digital instrument.

    The AR functionality enables visitors to
    place a digital representation of the instrument
    in their physical surroundings, where supported
    by their mobile device.

    The 3D representation provides an additional
    way to examine the physical characteristics
    of the instruments.

    This is a proposed application of virtual
    object manipulation and digital augmentation
    in museum learning.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}


    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- Additional information:}
    The 3D viewer should provide optional
    educational information about the selected
    instrument.

    This information could include its construction,
    characteristic sound, historical background
    and role within Basler Fasnacht.

    Audio recordings and written descriptions
    should complement the interactive 3D model.

    Visitors can access the additional information
    according to their own interests without being
    required to read it before exploring the instrument.

    This combines visitor-directed exploration
    with optional educational explanations.
    \cite[pp.~733--734]{franse_families_2021}
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}


    \item \textbf{Station 2 -- Connection between physical
    and digital exhibition:}
    The physical poster should provide the cultural
    context of Fasnacht music, while VirtuLearn
    allows visitors to explore selected instruments
    in greater detail.

    The QR code creates a direct transition
    from the physical exhibition to the digital
    music station.

    This allows visitors with limited waiting
    time to read the poster, while interested
    visitors can continue exploring the instruments
    through audio, 3D and AR.

    This is a proposed application of layered
    museum interaction and supplementary
    digital content.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}
    \cite[pp.~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Station 3 -- The three days of Fasnacht:}
    Present a physical timeline showing the main events
    of the three days of Basler Fasnacht.

    The timeline should include short explanations
    and photographs of important traditions, such
    as the Morgestraich, the Cortege, children's
    carnival, Guggenmusik and the Endstreich.

    The information should remain concise to allow
    visitors to understand the main traditions
    without requiring a long reading time.

    This follows the principle of layered
    information in museum exhibitions.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Station 3 -- Visitor-directed exploration:}
    Provide a QR code linking to the searchable
    digital Fasnacht collection in VirtuLearn.

    Visitors can independently search for
    additional objects according to their
    individual interests.

    The physical timeline introduces the cultural
    context, while the digital collection allows
    visitors to continue exploring beyond the
    information presented on the poster.

    This is a proposed application of open-ended
    exploration and visitor autonomy.
    \cite[pp.~708,~733]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Station 3 -- Digital cultural heritage:}
    Use the physical timeline as an introduction
    to the broader digital Fasnacht collection.

    Through VirtuLearn, visitors can discover
    additional objects and, where available,
    access images, descriptions, 3D models
    and AR visualizations.

    This provides a connection between the
    physical exhibition and the digital archive.

    It is inspired by the use of supplementary
    digital content in museum learning.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Station 4 -- VR introduction:}
    Provide a simple introduction to the VR headset and its
    controls before visitors enter VIRTUE.

    Instructions should be understandable without previous VR
    experience, reducing the initial difficulty of interacting
    with unfamiliar technology.
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Conceptual preparation for VIRTUE:}
    The VR introduction should explain not only the technology,
    but also what visitors will experience in VIRTUE.

    Visitors should know the main purpose of the virtual
    exhibition and what kind of interaction is expected.

    Whittaker describes onboarding as both technical and
    conceptual preparation.
    \cite[p.~88]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}
    \cite[pp.~95--96]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Station 4 -- Introduction to AR and VR:}
    Explain the differences between smartphone-based
    AR and immersive VR using a head-mounted display.

    Visitors should understand the different
    purposes of VirtuLearn and VIRTUE.

    The review distinguishes mobile AR applications
    from HMD-based AR and VR experiences.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Comfortable waiting area:}
    Create a welcoming environment where visitors can
    prepare for the VR experience.

    Consider appropriate seating, lighting and sufficient
    space for visitors waiting for their turn.

    This is inspired by the Limina Immersive Cinema
    case study, where particular attention was paid to
    visitor comfort before entering VR.
    \cite[p.~90]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Physical and digital interaction:}
    Where feasible, combine physical exhibition elements with
    digital interaction instead of relying exclusively on
    computer interfaces.

    In the evaluated museum, hands-on exhibits attracted
    visitors across different age and interest groups.
    \cite[p.~139]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Different amounts of guidance:}
    Visitors should not be forced to read detailed
    explanations before starting an activity.

    Instead, brief instructions should be available
    initially, with optional hints or additional information
    when visitors need further support.

    Franse et al. argue that guidance should respond
    to visitors' needs rather than follow a fixed
    intervention pattern.
    \cite[pp.~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{WebAR -- VirtuLearn}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Immediate interaction through QR codes:}
    Each QR code should open the corresponding activity or
    object directly, without requiring visitors to navigate
    through several menus.

    This decision is derived from the importance of a low
    threshold for interaction.
    \cite[p.~139]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Progressive exploration:}
    Start by showing the selected object or activity.
    Visitors can then access additional information,
    interactive details and AR visualization.

    This follows the principle of layering activities.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Free exploration:}
    In addition to predefined exhibition activities,
    visitors should be able to search the digital collection
    and explore objects according to their own interests.

    This design decision is inspired by the observed value
    of creative appropriation and exploratory interaction.
    \cite[pp.~140--141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Different levels of engagement:}
    Visitors can briefly inspect a 3D object or spend more
    time reading information, examining details or using AR.

    This allows VirtuLearn to support visitors with different
    interests and available time.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Optional personal content:}
    Consider allowing visitors to save or share a link to
    an object they discovered.

    This idea is inspired by the digital backpack concept,
    where visitors could access collected content after
    their museum visit.
    \cite[pp.~135--136]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Support VR preparation through VirtuLearn:}
    Consider including a short introduction to VIRTUE
    within the WebAR application.

    Visitors could access information about the headset,
    controllers and expected interactions before trying VR.

    This is a proposed application of Whittaker's concept
    of preparing audiences before entering an immersive
    experience.
    \cite[pp.~95--96]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Explore 3D objects before reading explanations:}
    Allow visitors to rotate, zoom and inspect digital
    Fasnacht objects before presenting detailed information.

    Selected models could include clickable features that
    reveal additional contextual explanations.

    This is a proposed transfer of open-ended
    investigation principles to interactive 3D objects,
    not an interaction directly tested by Franse et al.
    \cite[pp.~708,~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Optional hints instead of immediate answers:}
    During the Fasnacht figure matching activity,
    visitors could request hints before the correct
    answer is revealed.

    For example, an initial hint could draw attention
    to a characteristic feature of the mask without
    identifying the figure directly.

    This is inspired by the Describing Evidence
    guidance strategy, which directed visitors'
    attention to relevant observations.
    \cite[pp.~714,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Provide educational explanations:}
    After visitors have interacted with an object
    or completed a matching activity, VirtuLearn
    should offer concise factual explanations.

    The purpose is to support understanding and
    reduce possible misconceptions while preserving
    the opportunity for independent discovery.

    Franse et al. found that minimal guidance reduced
    some incorrect explanations during investigation.
    \cite[pp.~727,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Combine guided and unguided exploration:}
    Keep the curated exhibition activities separate
    from the possibility of freely exploring the
    digital collection.

    Visitors could follow a predefined activity
    or independently search for other Fasnacht objects.

    This is a proposed application of visitor autonomy
    and structured learning support to VirtuLearn.
    \cite[pp.~708,~711,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Avoid overwhelming visitors with guidance:}
    Additional information and hints should remain
    optional and should not interrupt the manipulation
    of 3D objects unnecessarily.

    The aim is to preserve the visitor's initiative
    while making relevant assistance accessible.

    This follows the paper's discussion of balancing
    visitor autonomy with educational guidance.
    \cite[pp.~711--712,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Mobile-based augmented reality:}
    VirtuLearn should make selected digital Fasnacht
    objects accessible through visitors' smartphones.

    Mobile devices were the most frequently used
    device category in the reviewed studies
    (28 of 51 studies).
    \cite[pp.~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Link physical stations to digital content:}
    QR codes at the physical stations should provide
    direct access to corresponding digital objects
    and educational activities.

    Zhou et al. describe museum applications in which
    visitors scan exhibits or QR codes to access
    additional multimedia information.
    \cite[p.~10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Distinguish 3D viewing from AR:}
    VirtuLearn should provide a conventional
    interactive 3D viewer and, where supported,
    a separate AR visualization mode.

    In AR mode, digital objects are integrated
    into the camera view of the physical environment.

    This design distinction follows the definition
    of AR presented in the review.
    \cite[p.~2]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Interaction with virtual objects:}
    Allow visitors to rotate, zoom and inspect
    selected 3D Fasnacht objects.

    Where feasible, specific parts of an object
    could be selected to reveal additional details.

    This is a proposed implementation inspired
    by the virtual object manipulation category.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Supplementary information for objects:}
    Provide contextual information about the
    displayed Fasnacht objects through text,
    images, audio or video.

    The review identifies supplementary information
    as the most frequently reported AR/VR design
    element in the selected museum studies.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Quiz-based activities:}
    Consider implementing short quizzes for
    selected exhibition topics, particularly
    the Fasnacht figure matching activity.

    The quiz should relate to the cultural
    information presented in the exhibition.

    The review identifies mobile quiz-based
    learning tasks as an existing AR design element.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Digital collection exploration:}
    The application should combine curated
    educational activities with a separate
    collection search.

    Visitors could use the vitrivr-based search
    to discover additional Fasnacht objects
    according to their interests.

    This is a project-specific design decision
    supporting exploration; the reviewed studies
    do not establish the effectiveness of
    vitrivr or collection search specifically.
    \cite[pp.~2,~12]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Optional user-generated content:}
    If implementation time permits, consider
    allowing visitors to create or save a
    personalized representation of an object.

    For example, visitors could select a
    variation of a digital Fasnacht mask
    or save a link to their exploration.

    User-generated content was identified as
    a design element in four reviewed studies.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Avoid unnecessary technical barriers:}
    The application should provide an understandable
    fallback when AR is unavailable on a device.

    Visitors should still be able to access
    the object's educational content and 3D viewer.

    This is a proposed accessibility measure,
    not a solution directly evaluated in the review.
    \cite[pp.~12--13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{VIRTUE -- Virtual Reality Exhibition}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Simple introduction:}
    The first interaction in VIRTUE should be easy to understand.
    Visitors should immediately recognize how to interact
    with the exhibition.

    This is derived from the importance of early successful
    interactions in public exhibition settings.
    \cite[pp.~139--141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Optional deeper exploration:}
    Visitors should be able to explore selected exhibition
    objects without being required to complete complicated
    interactions.

    Additional information can be available for visitors
    who want to spend more time in the VR exhibition.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Connection with physical stations:}
    Where possible, VIRTUE should present objects and themes
    already introduced in the waiting area.

    This is a proposed design decision to create a coherent
    visitor experience.

    \item \textbf{Technical onboarding:}
    Before entering VIRTUE, visitors should be shown how
    to wear the headset and operate the controllers.

    A short demonstration should explain the interactions
    required to navigate the exhibition.
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Clear assistance and stopping procedure:}
    Visitors should know how to request assistance,
    interrupt the experience or remove the headset.

    Exhibition staff should explain how they will help
    participants and under which circumstances physical
    assistance might be necessary.

    This is inspired by the procedures observed at
    Limina Immersive Cinema.
    \cite[pp.~90--91]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Safe and accessible VR environment:}
    Provide sufficient space for the movements required
    by VIRTUE.

    Consider whether seating or other physical support
    is appropriate for visitors with different abilities.

    The physical setup should correspond to the
    interaction requirements of the virtual experience.
    \cite[pp.~100--101]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Privacy during VR:}
    Consider separating the active VR area from the
    main waiting area using spatial arrangements or
    partial visual barriers.

    This may help visitors feel less exposed while
    wearing the headset.

    Privacy measures were used in the Limina Immersive
    Cinema case study.
    \cite[p.~90]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Secure personal belongings:}
    Consider providing a clearly designated place where
    visitors can safely leave their bags during VR.

    In the Limina case study, staff safeguarded visitors'
    belongings during the experience.
    \cite[p.~90]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Clearly defined staff responsibilities:}
    Exhibition staff should be prepared to manage
    the equipment, introduce visitors to the experience
    and ensure their physical and psychological safety.

    These correspond to three responsibilities identified
    by Whittaker.
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Independent exploration within VIRTUE:}
    Where supported by the existing VIRTUE platform,
    visitors should be able to explore selected virtual
    exhibition objects at their own pace.

    The exhibition should not require visitors to
    follow a rigid sequence of interactions.

    This is an adaptation of the open-ended museum
    exploration approach described by Franse et al.
    \cite[pp.~708,~733]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Optional contextual guidance in VR:}
    Where technically feasible, provide short contextual
    explanations for selected virtual objects.

    Visitors should be able to access these explanations
    without being required to read them before exploring.

    This is a proposed application of minimal guidance
    to the virtual exhibition.
    \cite[pp.~727,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Immersive exhibition environment:}
    Use VIRTUE to present selected Fasnacht objects
    within an immersive virtual museum environment.

    The review identifies virtual exhibition
    simulation as an established design element
    of AR and VR museum applications.
    \cite[pp.~6,~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Historical and cultural context:}
    Where supported by VIRTUE and the available
    collection, present objects in a context
    that helps visitors understand their
    cultural significance.

    The review describes the use of immersive
    environments and narrative simulations
    for historical museum learning.

    This is a proposed adaptation to the
    Fasnacht exhibition, not a requirement
    to reconstruct a complete historical scene.
    \cite[pp.~8--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Focus on meaningful object exploration:}
    Visitors should be able to inspect selected
    digital objects and access relevant information
    without completing unrelated gaming activities.

    This design choice prioritizes the learning
    objectives while using the affordances of
    an immersive exhibition.
    \cite[pp.~6,~12--13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Evaluate VR independently:}
    The effectiveness of the VIRTUE experience
    should not be assumed from the review's
    combined AR/VR effect sizes.

    Only one study in the meta-analysis
    compared VR with a non-VR condition,
    preventing separate effect estimates
    for AR and VR.
    \cite[p.~10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{External Screen -- Supporting Group Interaction}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Display the VR user's perspective:}
    Show the perspective of the person using VIRTUE on an
    external screen so accompanying visitors can observe
    the virtual exhibition.

    This is inspired by the TV Newsroom installation, where
    activities were visible to other visitors and became
    part of the public exhibition experience.
    \cite[pp.~140--142]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Encourage communication:}
    The screen should allow accompanying visitors to
    discuss the displayed objects and potentially guide
    or support the person using the VR headset.

    This applies the paper's findings on the importance
    of visibility and supporting group interaction.
    \cite[pp.~140--142]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Make the experience observable:}
    Position the screen so that several people can watch
    without obstructing the active VR user.

    The physical setup should provide sufficient space
    for spectators and encourage group engagement.
    \cite[p.~141]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Social potential of VR casting:}
    Displaying the headset perspective can help accompanying
    visitors understand what the VR user is experiencing.

    Whittaker describes social VR experiences in which
    users cast their headset view to a television so that
    others can follow the activity.

    This suggests a possible role for the external screen
    in supporting shared experiences.
    \cite[pp.~89--90]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Balance visibility and privacy:}
    The screen should support the audience experience
    without making the active VR user feel unnecessarily
    exposed.

    Consider informing visitors about the screen
    transmission and allowing them to decline public
    display of their headset perspective.

    This is a proposed design decision based on
    Whittaker's discussion of privacy, consent and
    social discomfort in public VR settings.
    \cite[pp.~90--91]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}
    \cite[p.~102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Separate spectators from the active VR area:}
    Consider positioning the screen in a place where
    accompanying visitors can watch without standing
    directly around the person wearing the headset.

    This is a proposed spatial solution to balance
    group participation and individual comfort.
    \cite[p.~100]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Encourage observations and discussions:}
    Consider using the external screen to encourage
    accompanying visitors to discuss the objects and
    scenes being explored in VIRTUE.

    For example, spectators could comment on or compare
    the displayed Fasnacht objects with those they
    encountered in the physical exhibition.

    This is a proposed transfer of the collaborative
    discussion observed by Franse et al.
    The study did not investigate external VR screens.
    \cite[pp.~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Supplementary display of VR content:}
    Consider using the external screen to make
    selected virtual exhibition content visible
    to visitors outside the headset.

    For example, the screen could show the
    current VIRTUE perspective or provide
    a preview of the virtual exhibition.

    This is a project-specific extension of
    the multimedia exhibition concept.
    The review does not directly evaluate
    the effects of VR casting on spectators.
    \cite[pp.~9--10]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Offboarding -- Returning from VIRTUE}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Gradual transition back to the physical space:}
    Visitors should have time to reorient themselves
    after removing the VR headset.

    Avoid requiring visitors to immediately move away
    from the VR area.

    The Limina case study describes a gradual transition
    back into the physical environment.
    \cite[p.~91]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Optional resting area:}
    Provide an opportunity for visitors to remain seated
    briefly after VR if needed.

    This may be particularly relevant for visitors who
    experience disorientation or discomfort.
    \cite[pp.~91--92]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Reflection after VR:}
    Provide visitors with an opportunity to reflect on
    their experience before leaving the exhibition.

    This could involve a short conversation, a
    questionnaire or revisiting selected content.

    The reflection activity is a proposed application
    of Whittaker's discussion of offboarding and
    the transition from virtual to physical experience.
    \cite[pp.~97--98]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Individual offboarding needs:}
    The transition out of VR should remain flexible.

    Some visitors may want to leave immediately,
    while others may need more time to recover,
    reflect or discuss their experience.
    \cite[pp.~98--103]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Optional shared reflection:}
    Visitors could be invited to discuss what they
    discovered in VIRTUE with accompanying visitors.

    Simple prompts could encourage them to describe
    interesting objects, compare observations or
    share newly discovered information.

    This is an adaptation of the collaborative reasoning
    observed at an open-ended science museum exhibit.
    \cite[pp.~721--723]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Reflect on the educational content:}
    After the VR experience, visitors could
    briefly reflect on what they discovered
    about the Basler Fasnacht.

    This could be encouraged through
    a short question or informal discussion.

    The idea is consistent with the experiential
    learning framework discussed by Zhou et al.,
    but the review does not specifically examine
    VR offboarding procedures.
    \cite[p.~2]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{Questionnaire and Evaluation}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Mixed-method evaluation:}
    Combine short questionnaires and interviews with
    observations of visitors interacting with the stations.

    Hornecker and Stifter used quantitative logfile
    analysis alongside interviews and observations.
    \cite[p.~137]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Different visitor groups:}
    Include participants of different ages and with
    different levels of experience using VR and AR.

    Examine whether the stations appeal to different
    visitor groups.
    \cite[pp.~137--139]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Engagement:}
    Investigate which stations attracted visitors,
    how long they interacted with them and whether
    they explored additional content.
    \cite[pp.~137--138]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Usability:}
    Observe whether visitors understand how to begin
    interacting with each station and identify situations
    where they become confused or abandon an activity.
    \cite[pp.~138--139]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Group interaction:}
    Observe whether visitors discuss the exhibits,
    collaborate on activities or watch others interact.
    \cite[pp.~140--142]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Qualitative feedback:}
    Ask visitors which activities they found most engaging,
    what they learned and what they would improve.

    Short semi-structured interviews can provide explanations
    for interaction patterns observed during the exhibition.
    \cite[p.~137]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Optional quantitative measurements:}
    If technically and ethically feasible, collect anonymous
    interaction data such as station visits, interaction
    duration or completed activities.

    Such data should be interpreted together with
    observations and qualitative feedback.
    \cite[pp.~137--138]{hornecker_learning_2006}

    \item \textbf{Effectiveness of VR onboarding:}
    Evaluate whether the introduction helped visitors
    understand the headset, controllers and interactions
    required within VIRTUE.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "The introduction prepared me well for the VR experience."

    This question is derived from the technical and
    conceptual goals of onboarding.
    \cite[p.~88]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Feeling of safety and comfort:}
    Investigate whether visitors felt physically and
    psychologically comfortable while using VR.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "I felt safe and comfortable during the VR experience."

    Whittaker emphasizes duty of care and the importance
    of considering individual physical and emotional needs.
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Privacy and social discomfort:}
    Investigate whether visitors felt uncomfortable
    being observed while using the headset.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "I felt comfortable with other people being present
    while I used the VR headset."

    This question is motivated by the contrast between
    the Limina and film festival case studies.
    \cite[pp.~90--91]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Physical discomfort and motion sickness:}
    Ask whether visitors experienced discomfort,
    dizziness or disorientation during the VR experience.

    Possible questionnaire question:
    "Did you experience any physical discomfort
    while using VR?"

    Such factors may influence how accessible and
    enjoyable the exhibition is for different visitors.
    \cite[pp.~88--89]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}
    \cite[p.~101]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Effectiveness of offboarding:}
    Investigate whether visitors felt comfortable
    returning to the physical environment after VR.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "I had enough time to readjust after removing
    the VR headset."

    This question is derived from Whittaker's
    discussion of offboarding.
    \cite[pp.~97--98]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{External screen and visitor experience:}
    Evaluate whether the external screen helped
    accompanying visitors understand and discuss
    the VR exhibition.

    Also investigate whether the person using the
    headset felt comfortable with the public display.

    This is a proposed evaluation of the balance
    between group interaction and user privacy.
    \cite[pp.~89--91]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Qualitative questions about preparation:}
    During short interviews, ask visitors whether
    anything was unclear before entering VIRTUE,
    whether they needed assistance and how the
    introduction could be improved.

    This would help evaluate the onboarding process
    from the visitor's perspective.
    \cite[pp.~101--102]{whittaker_onboarding_2025}

    \item \textbf{Observation of independent exploration:}
    Observe whether visitors independently explore
    the interactive objects or immediately search
    for instructions and explanations.

    Possible observation categories include:
    rotating objects, inspecting details, trying
    different options and requesting additional help.

    This is a proposed adaptation of the observational
    approach used by Franse et al. to evaluate
    visitor investigation.
    \cite[pp.~713--718]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Usefulness of hints and explanations:}
    Investigate whether optional hints helped visitors
    understand the activity or identify the correct
    Fasnacht figure.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "The hints and explanations helped me understand
    the Fasnacht figures."

    This is a proposed evaluation of the transferred
    minimal guidance approach.
    \cite[pp.~727,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Learning through discussion:}
    Observe whether visitors talk about the exhibits,
    compare their observations or explain their
    choices to accompanying visitors.

    Franse et al. assessed learning-related activity
    through the experiments and conversations of families.
    \cite[pp.~709--710,~717--718]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Short knowledge questions:}
    Consider including one or two optional questions
    to investigate whether visitors can identify
    Fasnacht figures or explain selected cultural details.

    These would provide an additional measure beyond
    self-reported learning.

    Franse et al. did not include a knowledge posttest
    and explicitly discuss this as a limitation.
    \cite[p.~732]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Avoid overreliance on interaction time:}
    Interaction duration alone should not be interpreted
    as evidence that visitors learned something.

    Consider examining the quality of interactions,
    explanations or answers in addition to duration.

    Franse et al. used measures of experiments and
    conversations and noted limitations of engagement
    time in their research setting.
    \cite[pp.~709--710,~734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Accessibility of guidance:}
    Investigate whether visitors with different
    levels of prior knowledge were able to participate
    independently and find useful assistance.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "I could explore the exhibition independently
    and find help when I needed it."

    This is a proposed evaluation of visitor autonomy
    and responsive guidance.
    \cite[pp.~711,~733--734]{franse_families_2021}

    \item \textbf{Separate learning and perceptions:}
    The evaluation should distinguish between
    knowledge acquired through the exhibition
    and visitors' subjective experience.

    Zhou et al. analyzed academic achievement
    and perceptions as separate outcomes.
    \cite[pp.~5,~11--12]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Self-reported learning:}
    Ask visitors whether they feel that the
    exhibition helped them understand the
    Basler Fasnacht better.

    Possible questionnaire statement:
    "The exhibition helped me learn something
    new about the Basler Fasnacht."

    This measures perceived learning rather
    than objectively demonstrated knowledge.
    \cite[p.~5]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Objective knowledge assessment:}
    Consider including one or two short questions
    that assess the knowledge presented at
    the interactive stations.

    For example:
    "Which traditional Fasnacht instrument
    did you hear at the music station?"

    Correct answers could indicate knowledge
    demonstrated after the exhibition,
    but without a baseline or control group
    they would not establish knowledge gains.

    The review distinguishes academic achievement
    from self-reported visitor perceptions.
    \cite[pp.~5,~11--13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Enjoyment and engagement:}
    Investigate visitors' satisfaction,
    engagement and enjoyment when interacting
    with VirtuLearn and VIRTUE.

    Possible questionnaire statements:
    "I enjoyed exploring the digital Fasnacht objects."
    "The interactive activities encouraged me
    to explore the exhibition further."

    These outcomes are included in the
    perception measures described by Zhou et al.
    \cite[pp.~5,~11--12]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Evaluation of AR and VR separately:}
    Record which technologies each participant
    actually used.

    Where possible, distinguish between
    visitors who used the interactive 3D viewer,
    the AR mode and the VIRTUE VR exhibition.

    This would allow the evaluation to identify
    which components contributed to the
    reported visitor experience.

    This is a proposed methodological choice,
    motivated by the limited separate evidence
    on VR effectiveness in the review.
    \cite[pp.~10--11]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Technology-related barriers:}
    Investigate whether visitors experienced
    technical problems, physical discomfort
    or difficulties using the equipment.

    Possible questionnaire question:
    "Did you experience any difficulties
    while using VirtuLearn or VIRTUE?"

    Zhou et al. identify dizziness and device
    burden as potential barriers that were
    not examined systematically in the review.
    \cite[p.~13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Visitor characteristics:}
    Consider recording age groups and
    previous experience with AR and VR.

    This may help identify differences
    in usability and perceived learning
    among visitor groups.

    The review recommends considering
    visitors' different cognitive abilities
    and technology experience.
    \cite[p.~13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Controlled evaluation if feasible:}
    If time and participant numbers allow,
    consider comparing a simple information
    presentation with an interactive
    VirtuLearn activity.

    For example, compare visitors using
    a conventional information page with
    visitors exploring a corresponding
    3D object and supplementary information.

    Such a comparison could provide stronger
    evidence for differences in learning
    outcomes than a single-group questionnaire.

    Zhou et al. highlight the need for
    more controlled experimental studies.
    \cite[p.~13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

    \item \textbf{Avoid overclaiming learning effects:}
    Positive questionnaire ratings should not
    automatically be interpreted as evidence
    of improved learning performance.

    The review reports favorable average
    effects on both academic achievement
    and perceptions, but its findings
    should not be treated as proof of
    effectiveness for this specific exhibition.

    The authors also acknowledge the limited
    number of studies in the meta-analysis.
    \cite[pp.~11--13]{zhou_meta-analytic_2022}

\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Station 1}
What:
- Interactive Quiz about Fasnacht characters and their historical meaning
\\
- Where visitors can guess the answers and then learn the solutions later through the WebAR
\\
- Visitors should be able to participate alone or discuss the answers in groups
\\
Why:
- We have active participation instead of passive reading (Hornecker \& Stifter, 2006)
\\
- Low threshold for interaction; short activities but immediate feedback (Hornecker \& Stifter, 2006)
\\
- We have something for larger groups as most museum visitors come in groups (Hornecker \& Stifter, 2006)
\\
- Indpendent exploration (Franse et al., 2021)
\\
\subsubsection{Station 2: WebAR Exploration}

What:
- QR code to open the WebAR application
\\
- Select different Fasnacht objects
\\
- Rotate, zoom and examine 3D models
\\
- Here is could also be the Hologram
\\
- AR placement through camera
\\
- Additional information about cultural and historical meaning
\\
Why:
- Hands-on activities attracted visitor engagement (Hornecker \& Stifter, 2006)
\\
- Visitors should be able to explore independently (Franse et al., 2021)
\\
- Optional guidance could support understanding (Franse et al., 2021)
\\
- Different levels of engagement: quick exploration or additional information. (Layering) (Hornecker \& Stifter, 2006)
\\
\subsubsection{Station 3: VR Onboarding}

What:
- Short introduction to the VR headset
\\
- Explanation of the controller functions needed in VIRTUE (maybe Video)
\\
- How to put on and adjust the headset (maybe Video)
\\
- How to ask staff for help or stop the experience
\\
- Basic safety instructions
\\

Why:
- VR onboarding should begin before entering the virtual environment (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Technical instructions and staff assistance are important parts of onboarding (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Different visitors have different levels of VR experience (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Clear instructions and a comfortable environment can help address visitor uncertainty (Whittaker, 2025)


\subsubsection{VIRTUE: Protected VR Area}

What:
- Dedicated area for the VR exhibition
\\
- Enough space to not endanger someone
\\
- Some visual separation from waiting visitors (important)
\\
- Staff supervision and assistance
\\
- Possible storage area for personal belongings

Why:
- Not just te VR experience also physical surroundings influence the VR experience (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Physical comfort, privacy and staff assistance should be considered (Whittaker, 2025)
\\

\subsubsection{Station 4: VR Offboarding and Reflection}

What:
- Good to discuss the VR experience with others
\\
- Short reflection questions about what visitors saw or learned
\\
- Optional connection to related Fasnacht objects or WebAR content
\\
Why:
- Offboarding is part of the overall VR experience (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Visitors should have time to readjust to the physical environment (Whittaker, 2025)
\\
- Reflection and discussion are proposed educational extensions; Whittaker does not directly test their learning effectiveness
\\
